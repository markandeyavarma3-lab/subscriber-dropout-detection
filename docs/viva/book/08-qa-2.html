SECTION EMetrics and evaluation

Why not use accuracy?core

Only 1.5% of subscribers churn, so predicting "nobody churns" is 98.5% accurate and useless. Accuracy rewards ignoring the rare class. My model's accuracy is 95.6%, lower than that useless baseline, because it deliberately flags people.

Define precision and recall.core

Precision: of those flagged, the fraction who really churn (TP ÷ (TP + FP)). Recall: of those who really churn, the fraction flagged (TP ÷ (TP + FN)). At the 0.05 threshold: precision 7.6% (85 of 1,125 flagged), recall 16.8% (85 of 506 churners).

What is F1?

The harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2PR ÷ (P + R). It's high only when both are high, and treats a missed churner and a false alarm as equally bad. Here it's 0.104 at 0.05. That equal treatment is its weakness when the two mistakes cost different amounts, which is why the cost audit exists.

What is ROC-AUC?core

The area under the curve of true-positive rate against false-positive rate across all thresholds. It equals the probability that the model scores a random churner above a random non-churner. 0.5 is random; 1.0 is perfect. This model: 0.835.

What is PR-AUC, and why is it your main metric?core

The area under the precision–recall curve across all thresholds. A random model's PR-AUC equals the positive rate (here 0.015). It's built from precision, which is dominated by the rare class, so it reflects how useful the flags really are when churners are rare. ROC-AUC uses the false-positive rate, which divides by the huge number of non-churners and so hides how many false alarms you'd send. Mine is 0.073: about 4.8× random.

Tell me about ROC-AUC going up while PR-AUC went down.core

Moving from synthetic data (about 20% churn) to real KKBox (1.5%), ROC-AUC rose from 0.674 to 0.835 while PR-AUC fell from 0.345 to 0.073. ROC-AUC said "better"; PR-AUC said "much harder", which is the truth for the business, because with rarer churners far more flags are false alarms. A gate on ROC-AUC would have claimed an improvement at exactly the wrong moment. That's why the gate uses PR-AUC.

PR-AUC 0.073 sounds terrible. Is your model any good?coretricky

Compare it with the baseline, not with 1.0. Random gets 0.015, so the model is about 4.8× better; its flags are 5× richer in real churners than random picks. But honestly, it's modest: it catches 1 churner in 6. The data lacks strong signals (two inputs are always zero), and my stricter churn definition leaves few positives. The project's contribution is mainly the engineering around the model, and the model can be swapped through the gate.

Show me the confusion matrix.

At threshold 0.05, on 33,042 test subscribers: 85 true positives, 1,040 false positives, 421 false negatives, 31,496 true negatives. 1,125 flagged, of whom 85 churned; 506 churned in total.

Why do validation and test scores differ (PR-AUC 0.052 vs 0.073)?tricky

They're different months with different churn rates (1.43% vs 1.53%) and only about 500 positives each, so PR-AUC is noisy between periods. ROC-AUC is almost identical (0.834 vs 0.835). The variation is a reason for the promotion margin: small differences between runs can be noise.

What is a baseline, and what's yours?

The simplest reference a model must beat to be worth anything. Here: random guessing (PR-AUC 0.015) and "predict nobody churns" (98.5% accuracy, zero recall). The model beats random by 4.8× on PR-AUC. A good next baseline would be a simple rule, like "inactive for 30+ days", to show how much the model adds over common sense.

What is the Brier score?

The mean squared difference between predicted probability and outcome (0 or 1); lower is better. It measures accuracy and calibration of the probabilities together. Here it's 0.0146.

SECTION FThresholds, costs, calibration and fairness

What is the decision threshold, and why 0.05?core

The probability above which a subscriber is flagged. 0.5 would flag almost nobody at a 1.5% churn rate. 0.05 is the value that maximised F1 on the validation set, searched from 0.05 to 0.95. It sits at the bottom of that range, so F1 would have liked it even lower.

Is 0.05 the best threshold?tricky

Not by cost. The cost audit prices each mistake and finds the cheapest threshold is 0.43, about £16.5k cheaper on the test set, because at 1.5% churn and 30% offer efficacy almost nobody is worth an offer. But those costs are placeholders. The audit reports the gap rather than silently changing the threshold, because the right choice depends on real business costs.

Explain your cost model.core

A missed churner costs £240 (about a year of revenue). An offer costs £20 and saves 30% of real churners. So flagging a churner costs £20 + 70% × £240 = £188 (the offer, plus the loss when it fails), flagging a non-churner costs £20, and missing a churner costs £240. Flagging is worth it when p × (240 − 188) > (1 − p) × 20, which gives p > 0.278. All of these are in params.yaml and all are assumptions.

Why does the cost-optimal policy send only 8 offers?tricky

Because an offer only helps if the subscriber would really churn and the offer works (30%). With a 1.5% base rate, very few subscribers have a churn probability high enough that the expected saving beats a £20 offer. The honest conclusion under these assumptions is that retention offers are barely worth it for this population, not that the model is wrong.

What does "calibrated" mean? Is your model calibrated?core

A model is calibrated if its probabilities mean what they say: among subscribers given 5%, about 5% churn. It matters because the probability feeds cost decisions. Expected calibration error is 0.004, which is very good. It slightly under-predicts on average (1.16% predicted vs 1.53% actual).

What is expected calibration error?

Predictions are grouped into bins by probability. In each bin, compare the average predicted probability with the actual churn rate, and take the weighted average of those gaps. 0 means perfectly calibrated.

Did you calibrate the model?tricky

Every run tries isotonic calibration, fitted on the validation set, and reports whether it would help. It would improve the error by a further 0.003, from an already-small 0.004. It's reported, not applied: the raw model is already well calibrated, and applying it is a one-line decision if that changes.

How do you check fairness?core

The audit compares the model across plan_type groups (minimum 30 members each), on selection rate (who gets flagged) and on performance (ROC-AUC, recall). A ratio below 0.8 between worst and best group is flagged: the "four-fifths rule", a convention from US employment law.

Your fairness audit fails. What does that mean?coretricky

On the standard plan (949 subscribers in the test set) the model's ROC-AUC is 0.54, close to random, far below the other plans. So it's measurably worse at finding churners on that plan. The likely cause is too few standard-plan subscribers to learn from. It's written to the report, shown in Grafana, and fires the FairnessDisparity alert. Reported and alerted, not hidden, and not yet fixed.

"How would you fix it?" More standard-plan data, a per-plan threshold, or a model that learns plan-specific patterns. Then the gate and the audit would confirm it on the next run.

Why audit fairness by plan and not by age or gender?

Plan type is a model input and a business segment, and every subscriber has one. The members file has age and gender, but they're not model inputs and the KKBox values are often missing or implausible (age 0, for example). Auditing on protected attributes would need cleaner data; it's the right extension where it's available.

What is the capacity constraint?

A real retention team can only contact so many people. If the cost-optimal policy needs more offers than the budget allows, the model contacts the top-k by risk and the report prices the subscribers left uncovered. No cap is set for KKBox because the cost-optimal policy only needs 8 offers.

SECTION GExplainability

What is SHAP?core

SHapley Additive exPlanations: a method from cooperative game theory for splitting a prediction among the features. Each feature's SHAP value is its average marginal contribution across all possible orderings of the features. For tree models, TreeExplainer computes them exactly and quickly.

Why is additivity important?core

Because it guarantees the explanation matches the prediction: base value plus every contribution equals exactly what the model predicted (in log-odds). So the reasons shown can't contradict the score. A test checks this for every row. Rule-based explanations don't have this property; they describe the input without knowing what the model did.

Why are SHAP values in log-odds?

Gradient boosting for classification works internally in log-odds and converts to a probability at the end with the logistic function. Additivity holds in log-odds; in probability space contributions don't add up simply. The dashboard shows the contribution sizes and the final probability.

How do you turn SHAP values into a sentence?

Contributions for related columns are summed into ten concepts (for example, tenure_days and tenure_months become "tenure"). That's valid because SHAP values add. Concepts are ranked by absolute size, and the top ones are phrased from the actual input: "relies on discounts (2 in the last 6 months)".

Feature importance vs SHAP: what's the difference?core

Feature importance is global: how much a feature matters across the whole dataset, with no direction. SHAP is local: how much each feature pushed this prediction, and in which direction. A retention agent needs the local view: why this subscriber.

What happens if SHAP fails?

The prediction still goes out, with a rule-based explanation and explanation_method: "rules", so the client knows which kind it got. An explanation is a feature of a prediction, never a precondition for one.

What are the counterfactual levers on the dashboard?core

SHAP says what the model leaned on, not what a team could change. So for each lever the dashboard sends a second real request with one field changed and shows the new probability. For the at-risk example, turning auto-renew off takes 60% to 0.24%, and stopping discounts takes it to 0.79%. They're the model's actual answers, not inferences.

Should the business turn off auto-renew for everyone, then?tricky

No. That's exactly the trap. The model learned that auto-renew is associated with churn in KKBox, because it goes with cheap short plans. Switching it off wouldn't change who those subscribers are. A counterfactual shows what the model would predict, not what would happen in the world. Acting on it would need an experiment.

Tell me about the explanation bug you fixed.

A subscriber with auto-renew off was explained as "auto-renew enabled". The wording followed the direction of the SHAP effect rather than the input's value. For a number that's harmless ("inactive for 45 days" and "last seen 45 days ago" are both true), but for a yes/no field it states something false. It now states the actual value, with a test.

SECTION HRegistry, promotion and shadow scoring

What does MLflow do in your project?core

Two things. Tracking records every training run: parameters, metrics, the model file. The registry stores models as numbered versions under one name, with aliases (@champion, @challenger) that say which version is live.

What is a model registry, and why do you need one?core

A catalogue of trained models with versions and metadata. It gives one authoritative answer to "which model is in production, and how was it made?", and it makes promotion and rollback an explicit, recorded action instead of copying files around.

How does promotion work?core

A new model is registered as @challenger. The gate scores it and the current champion on the same held-out data with PR-AUC. If the challenger wins by at least 0.005, the @champion alias moves to it. If there's no champion yet, the first model is promoted unopposed. Every decision is recorded.

Why a margin of 0.005 instead of "any improvement"?core

Two equivalent models differ by random noise. With zero margin the gate would promote on noise about half the time, and the champion would flip back and forth forever. A margin means only a real improvement is enough to move production.

Why compare both models on the same data?tricky

Comparing a challenger's fresh score with a champion's score from an old run compares two different datasets. The difference could be the data, not the model. That's a reliable way to promote a worse model. Scoring both on the same rows in the same process makes the comparison fair.

How do you roll back a bad model?core

Move the @champion alias back to the previous version. Serving loads whatever the alias points to, so nothing is rebuilt or redeployed.

Why aliases rather than MLflow stages like "Production"?

MLflow 3 removed stages; aliases replace them. They're also more flexible: any number of named pointers (champion, challenger) rather than a fixed set of stages.

How do you know the gate works?core

CI proves it. The pipeline job trains twice on identical data; the second model is identical to the champion, so it must be rejected, and the job fails if it isn't. A rejected challenger is the system working correctly, so it's an info alert, never a page.

What is shadow scoring?core

When a distinct challenger exists, every live request is scored by both models, but only the champion's answer is returned. The challenger's answers are compared: agreement rate, how the number of flagged subscribers would change, probability divergence, errors and latency. It judges the challenger on real traffic with zero risk to users.

Shadow scoring vs A/B testing?tricky

In A/B testing, a share of real users actually receive the new model's decisions, so you can measure real outcomes (did they churn?) but users bear the risk. In shadow scoring nobody receives the challenger's decisions: zero risk, but you only learn how it would behave, not the outcomes. Shadow first, then an A/B test, is a common, safe sequence.

What if the challenger crashes during shadow scoring?

The champion's answer is returned regardless, and the error is counted. A rising count fires the ShadowChallengerErroring alert, which is exactly what should block that challenger's promotion.

Why does the dashboard show shadow scoring as "IDLE"?

Because there's currently no distinct challenger: the only registered model is the champion. It's built and tested (CI trains a distinct challenger and checks shadow scoring works); in the demo it honestly reports "no challenger" rather than a misleading 0%.

SECTION IServing and the API

Why FastAPI?core

Typed request validation through Pydantic, automatic interactive documentation at /docs, high performance, and a small amount of code. Invalid input is rejected with a clear 422 error before it reaches the model.

Walk me through a prediction request.core

POST /predict with nine fields. Pydantic validates types, ranges and that last activity isn't longer ago than tenure. The features go through the saved pipeline; the champion returns a probability; it's mapped to low, medium or high using the threshold. SHAP computes contributions and a sentence is built. If a challenger exists it scores in shadow. Metrics update, and the response returns the probability, label, band, threshold, explanation, attributions and method.

Difference between /health and /ready?core

/health is liveness: is the process running? If not, restart it. /ready is readiness: can it serve, meaning is a model loaded? If not, stop sending it traffic, but don't kill it. Kubernetes uses these for its liveness and readiness probes.

What happens if no model is loaded?

The service still starts and answers /health; /ready reports not ready, and /predict returns 503 Service Unavailable with a clear message rather than a 500 crash. The ModelNotLoaded alert fires after five minutes.

How are the risk bands defined?

Low: below the threshold (5%). High: above the point halfway from the threshold to 100%, about 52.5%. Medium: in between.

How does the API know which model to load?

The setting SDD_MODEL_SOURCE: registry loads the @champion from MLflow; local loads the saved artifact; auto tries the registry first and falls back to local. In the Docker stack, the promoted artifact is mounted read-only and loaded locally.

Why is there a batch endpoint?

Scoring many subscribers in one call (1 to 1,000) avoids per-request overhead: one validation pass, one model call. It's how a nightly list or the stream scorer scores efficiently.

How would this scale to many requests?

The API is stateless apart from in-memory metrics, so it scales horizontally: more replicas behind a load balancer. The Kubernetes manifests do exactly this with an autoscaler from 2 to 10 replicas at 70% CPU. Prediction is fast (milliseconds); SHAP is the heavier part, and could be made optional per request if needed.

Is the API secure?tricky

It validates all input and runs as a non-root user in the container, but it has no authentication: it's designed for a trusted internal network. For real use it would sit behind an API gateway with authentication, rate limiting and HTTPS. No secrets are stored in the repository, and a test fails the build if credentials appear in the alerting config.

How did you build and test the dashboard?

It's a single self-contained HTML page served by the API: no build step, no external libraries. Tests check its structure against the API schema, and it was verified by rendering it in headless Chrome and inspecting screenshots. That caught problems the structural tests missed, including a version that rendered completely blank.
