CHAPTER 7Training and evaluating the model

A deliberately simple, well-understood model, judged with metrics that suit a rare event, and audited for whether it is actually fit for the decision it drives.

The model

A scikit-learn gradient boosting classifier: an ensemble of shallow decision trees, each built to correct the errors of the ones before it.

ParameterValueWhy
n_estimators300Enough trees to learn the signal at a low learning rate
learning_rate0.05Small steps generalise better than large ones
max_depth3Shallow trees capture interactions without memorising
subsample0.9Each tree sees 90% of rows, which adds randomness and reduces overfitting
min_samples_leaf20No leaf is built on a handful of subscribers

Why gradient boosting? It's among the strongest methods for tabular data with mixed numeric and categorical columns. It captures non-linear effects (risk that rises sharply after a few inactive weeks) without hand-built features. And it works with SHAP's exact tree explainer, so every prediction can be explained exactly. All parameters live in params.yaml, so changing one is a versioned, reviewable change.

Why accuracy is useless here

Only about 1.5% of subscribers cancel in a 30-day window. A "model" that predicts nobody ever churns is right 98.5% of the time, and completely useless. This model's accuracy is 95.6%, lower than that useless baseline, because it deliberately flags people. Accuracy rewards ignoring the rare class, so it's never used to judge anything in this project.

PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and the finding that shaped the project

ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks a random churner above a random non-churner. It uses the false-positive rate, which divides by the huge number of non-churners, so it barely notices how rare churn is. PR-AUC (area under the precision–recall curve) is built from precision: "of the people I flagged, how many really churn?". That is dominated by the rare class. A random model's PR-AUC equals the churn rate.

The project saw the difference directly. Moving from synthetic data (about 20% churn) to real KKBox data (1.5% churn):

MetricSynthetic dataReal KKBoxDirection
ROC-AUC0.6740.835Up
PR-AUC0.3450.073Down, sharply

ROC-AUC said the model got better. PR-AUC said the problem got much harder, and PR-AUC is the one that matches the business reality: with rarer churners, far more of the people you flag are false alarms. If the promotion gate had used ROC-AUC, it would have reported an improvement at the exact moment the model became harder to act on. That's why the gate uses PR-AUC. The decision was made before the real data arrived, and the real data confirmed it.

The surprising one: auto-renew raises risk

Intuition says auto-renew should lower churn risk. On KKBox it raises it: on an otherwise identical subscriber, turning auto-renew on moves the prediction from 0.0024 to 0.6037. In this data, auto-renew goes with the cheap, short plans that churn most. The model learned a real association in this data, not a cause. That's the honest answer, and a good example of why models need explanations and human judgement.

Choosing the decision threshold

The model outputs a probability; a threshold turns it into a decision (flag or not). The default 0.5 would flag almost no one when churn is 1.5%. The served threshold is 0.05. It's the value that maximised F1 on the validation set, searched over 0.05 to 0.95 in steps of 0.01. It sits at the very bottom of that range, which means F1 would have preferred it even lower.

The decision-quality audit

Every training run writes a report asking whether the model is fit for the decision it drives, not just whether its metrics look good. Four parts.

1 · Cost

Each outcome is given a cost (in params.yaml; these are placeholder assumptions, not real business figures):

Missed churner (false negative)£240: about a year of lost revenue
Retention offer£20
Offer efficacy30%: an offer saves 3 in 10 real churners
Flagged churner (true positive)£20 + 70% × £240 = £188
Flagged non-churner (false positive)£20: the wasted offer

Flagging someone is worth it only when the expected saving beats the offer's cost: p × (240 − 188) > (1 − p) × 20, which gives p > 0.278. Searching thresholds on the test set, the cheapest is 0.43: total cost £121,312, against £137,820 at the served 0.05, so £16,508 cheaper. At 0.43 the model sends only 8 offers out of 33,042.

The honest reading

At a 1.5% churn rate, and with an offer that only works 30% of the time, almost no one is risky enough to be worth an offer. So the cost-optimal policy is "send almost nothing". F1 chose 0.05; the cost audit prefers 0.43. The audit reports this rather than silently changing the threshold, because the right answer depends on real costs the project doesn't have. Grafana shows it as "savings left on the table".

2 · Calibration

A model is calibrated when a predicted 5% really happens about 5% of the time. That matters whenever a probability drives a money decision. Expected calibration error is 0.004, and the Brier score is 0.0146, both very good. On average it predicts 1.16% against an actual 1.53%, so it slightly under-predicts. Isotonic calibration is tried as a diagnostic on every run: fitted on the validation set, it would improve the error by a further 0.003. It's reported, not applied; the raw model is already well calibrated.

3 · Fairness

The audit compares performance across plan_type groups (groups need at least 30 members, and a ratio below 0.8 between the worst and best group is flagged: the US "four-fifths rule" from employment law, a convention rather than a law of nature). It fails. On the standard plan (949 subscribers in the test set) the model's ROC-AUC is 0.54, close to random, against much higher on the other plans. The selection rates also differ widely. The likely cause is too few standard-plan subscribers to learn from. The failure is written to the report, exported to Prometheus, shown as "DISPARITY" in Grafana, and fires the FairnessDisparity alert. It's reported, not hidden.

4 · Capacity

A real retention team can only contact so many people. The cost model supports a capacity cap (max_offer_rate): if the cost-optimal policy needs more offers than the budget allows, the threshold rises to the top-k subscribers and the report prices the shortfall. No cap is set for KKBox, since the cost-optimal policy only needs 8 offers.

Say this

"Accuracy is useless at 1.5% churn: predicting 'nobody churns' is 98.5% accurate. I use PR-AUC, and the real data proved why: ROC-AUC went up while PR-AUC fell. The model gets 0.073, about 5× better than random. It's modest, and I say so. Every run is audited: it's well calibrated, the cost audit prefers a stricter threshold under placeholder costs, and the fairness audit fails on one plan, which is reported and alerted on."

CHAPTER 8The registry and the promotion gate

No model reaches production by accident. Every model is recorded, and the only way to become the live model is to beat the current one on evidence.

MLflow: tracking and registry

Every training run is logged to MLflow: its parameters, metrics and the model itself. Models are registered under one name, subscriber-dropout-classifier, as numbered versions. Two aliases say which version plays which role:

  • @champion: the version serving production traffic.
  • @challenger: the newest candidate.

Serving loads "whatever @champion points to". Promoting a model means moving an alias; rolling back means moving it back. No files are copied and nothing is redeployed. (MLflow 3 removed the older "Staging/Production" stages altogether; aliases replace them.)

The gate

src/registry/promote.py decides. It has three properties:

  1. Comparable: champion and challenger are scored on the same held-out data, in the same process. Comparing a challenger's fresh test score with a champion's score from months ago measures two different datasets, and that's a reliable way to promote a worse model.
  2. The right metric: PR-AUC, for the reasons in Chapter 7.
  3. A margin: the challenger must win by at least 0.005. Two equivalent models differ only by noise; with a zero margin, the gate would promote on that noise about half the time, and the registry would flip back and forth forever.

If there's no champion yet, the first model is promoted unopposed. Every decision (promoted or rejected, with both scores and the required margin) is recorded in the run report.

Proved in CI, not just claimed

The pipeline job in CI trains twice on identical data. The second model is identical to the champion, so it must be rejected, and CI fails if it isn't. A rejected challenger isn't a failure: it's the gate doing its job, so it raises an info alert, never a page.

Shadow scoring: judging a challenger on real traffic

Test-set scores are one thing; live behaviour is another. When a distinct challenger is registered, the API scores every request with both models but returns only the champion's answer. The challenger's answer is recorded and compared:

  • Agreement rate: how often both models make the same flag / don't-flag decision.
  • Outreach volume change: how many more or fewer people the challenger would flag, which tells the business how the campaign would change on promotion.
  • Divergence: the typical difference between the two probabilities.
  • Errors and latency: a challenger that crashes or is slow is caught before it matters.

Shadow scoring never affects the response. If the challenger fails, the champion's answer still goes out and the error is counted. A verdict needs at least 200 comparisons. Right now no distinct challenger exists, so the dashboard honestly shows "IDLE" and "no challenger".

Say this

"Every run is logged in MLflow. The live model is whichever version holds the @champion alias. A new model only takes over if it beats the champion on PR-AUC, on the same data, by at least 0.005, so noise can't flip it. Rollback is just moving the alias back. CI proves an identical challenger gets rejected. And shadow scoring lets a challenger be judged on live traffic without ever answering a real request."
